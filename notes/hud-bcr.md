# Ex Ante Cost–Benefit Estimates for the 1994 HUD Wind Standards (24 CFR Part 3280): A Benchmarking Guide

## TL;DR
- HUD's own ex ante Regulatory Impact Analysis for the 1994 wind rule (59 FR 2456) projected an **annual net benefit of ~$32.1 million** ($83.8M annual benefits vs. $51.7M annual costs), with per-unit production-cost increases of roughly **$1,500–$2,700** and per-unit lifetime private benefits of about **$1,516 (Zone II) to $2,022 (Zone III)** discounted at 7% — an implied benefit-cost ratio of roughly **1.6:1**.
- The empirical (ex post) literature specific to the 1994 standards is **thin**: the strongest peer-reviewed study (Simmons & Sutter 2008) found post-1994 homes were 68% less likely to be leveled than 1976–1994 homes, and HUD/FEMA damage assessments after the 2004 hurricanes found essentially zero serious damage to post-1994 units — but no published study has monetized flood/wind insurance-claim differentials by manufactured-housing vintage, which is precisely the user's contribution.
- The best external benchmarks are the NIBS "Natural Hazard Mitigation Saves" BCRs — **$10 saved per $1 for adopting current hurricane-wind code requirements and $5 per $1 for exceeding them (IBHS FORTIFIED)** — and the FEMA/NIBS 2005 grant BCR of ~4:1 overall; these are far higher than HUD's own ~1.6:1 and provide a plausible upper bound.

## Key Findings

**1. The 1994 RIA is the central ex ante document, and its headline numbers survive.** HUD prepared a Regulatory Impact Analysis under E.O. 12866 but did not print the numeric tables in the Federal Register; the figures survive in the litigation record (*Florida Manufactured Housing Ass'n v. Cisneros*, 53 F.3d 1565 (11th Cir. 1995)) and are re-presented in HUD's 2006 methodological guidebook *Housing Impact Analysis*. HUD projected annual benefits of $83.8M, annual costs of $51.7M, and net annual benefit of $32.1M. Benefits were assumed to eliminate 75% of wind damage in Zone II and 83% in Zone III.

**2. Per-unit figures.** For a single-section home in Wind Zone III, HUD estimated a production-cost increase of $2,119 but a consumer price increase of only $1,177 (implying ~56% pass-through). The 2006 re-statement gives production-cost increases ranging from $1,492 (single-section, Zone II) to $2,722 (multi-section, Zone III), and lifetime private benefits (7% discount) of $1,516 (Zone II) and $2,022 (Zone III). Standards were deliberately set so that marginal production cost ≈ expected private benefit.

**3. Discount rate, home life, monetization.** Benefits were discounted at 7% per year over the home's service life; a value-of-statistical-life figure was applied to the death/injury reduction (yielding small per-unit mortality benefits of $39 Zone II / $43 Zone III). Public benefits from reduced FEMA disaster spending were $782–$1,063 (Zone II) and $1,043–$1,418 (Zone III).

**4. Industry counter-estimates were roughly double HUD's.** The Manufactured Housing Institute argued production costs would be about twice HUD's estimate and that, combined with lower benefit estimates, the rule would yield a net societal loss of ~$61.3M/year. The Eleventh Circuit sided with HUD.

**5. Ex post empirical evidence is genuinely sparse and mostly non-monetized.** The literature confirms the standards "worked" physically but rarely translates this into dollars or insurance-claim differentials.

## Details

### A. The ex ante Regulatory Impact Analysis (1994) — primary source
- **Rule:** "Manufactured Home Construction and Safety Standards on Wind Standards; Final Rule," 59 FR 2456, published January 14, 1994; effective July 13, 1994; proposed rule at 58 FR 19536 (April 14, 1993). Created Wind Zones I/II/III keyed to ASCE 7-88 (design wind speeds 100 mph Zone II, 110 mph Zone III, 50-year recurrence, Exposure C).
- **Net benefit:** +$32.1M/year ($83.8M benefits − $51.7M costs). HUD did not state a formal BCR; the implied ratio is ~1.62:1. Year dollars: early-1990s (1992–1993).
- **Damage-reduction assumption:** 75% of wind damage eliminated in Zone II, 83% in Zone III.
- **Per-unit production cost:** $1,492–$2,722 depending on section count and zone; single-section Zone III = $2,119 (with $1,177 passed to consumers). Material costs were multiplied by an industry factor of 2.22 to capture full production/management cost.
- **Pass-through:** estimated at 56%, rising toward 100% to the degree the zones are true submarkets and manufacturers can shift production across zones.
- **Per-unit benefits (7% discount, over service life):** private $1,516 (Zone II) / $2,022 (Zone III); public (reduced FEMA spending) $782–$1,063 / $1,043–$1,418; mortality $39 / $43.
- **Aggregate housing-market impact** (per HUD's 2006 re-analysis): ~$54M/year, below the $100M "economically significant" threshold. The 1994 preamble states the rule was initially projected potentially above $100M but concluded to be below it, "with benefits that outweigh the costs."
- **Supporting engineering:** NIST report NISTIR 5189 (Richard D. Marshall, May 1993) recommended ASCE 7-88 as the basis; NISTIR 5370 (Marshall, Nov. 1994) estimated probabilities of failure pre- vs. post-standard.

*Sources: 59 FR 2456 (govinfo.gov, FR Doc. 94-825); HUD, Housing Impact Analysis (huduser.gov, Jan. 2006), §4.2.2; Florida Mfd. Housing Ass'n v. Cisneros, 53 F.3d 1565 (11th Cir. 1995).*

### B. The modern methodological template (Cityscape)
- **Michael Hollar, "Regulatory Impact Analysis of Manufactured Home Construction and Safety Standards," Cityscape 25(1) (2023)** (huduser.gov, vol25num1/ch16.pdf), analyzing the July 19, 2022 proposed rule (4th/5th MHCC sets). This is the current template the user should emulate: it uses two reference homes (493 sq ft single-section; 2,000 sq ft double-section), assumes annual production of 105,400 units, treats costs as one-time upfront and benefits as annual streams over the home's life, and reports net present value at both **3% and 7% discount rates** over **30 and 45 years** (manufactured-home expected life stated as 30–55 years).
- The 2022 proposed rule (87 FR 43828, FR-6233) also updates the wind reference standard from ASCE 7-88 to ASCE 7-05 (three-second gust: 140 mph Zone II, 150 mph Zone III), per MHCC consensus.
- Earlier HUD RIAs (e.g., the 2010 rule at 75 FR 40338, and the 2005/2013/2021 MHCC rulemakings at 70 FR 72023, 78 FR 73965, 86 FR 2496) provide additional worked examples using the same cost/benefit-stream architecture.

### C. HUD-commissioned and FEMA post-storm performance studies (ex post, physical not monetized)
- **HUD Charley study** — "Assessment of Damage to Manufactured Homes Caused by Hurricane Charley" (huduser.gov, HurricaneCharley04.pdf): objective statistical sample of a representative set of homes; post-July-13-1994 homes performed markedly better than pre-1994 units. Follows prior HUD studies McKee & Crandell (1999) and Crandell & McKee (2000).
- **Florida DHSMV assessment** (reported via UF Shimberg Center, "Hurricanes and Manufactured Housing"): of 11,800 units inspected after Charley across seven counties, 2,422 (20.5%) were destroyed or damaged beyond repair; of 2,883 post-1994 HUD Code homes observed, **none were seriously damaged**. A post-Andrew unit typically had ~10 tie-downs versus as many as 45 for a post-1999 Florida installation.
- **FEMA MAT report FEMA 490** ("Summary Report on Building Performance: 2004 Hurricane Season," fema.gov): manufactured-home performance was "a function of unit age and of the regulations under which they were constructed and installed."
- **FEMA FIA-22** ("Building Performance: Hurricane Andrew in Florida," Feb. 1993): documented that manufactured-home breakup generated damaging windborne debris to downwind structures.
- **Grosskopf (2005)**, *Journal of Emergency Management* 3(5):27–32 (doi:10.5055/jem.2005.0049): after-action reports from Charley/Frances/Ivan/Jeanne indicate all post-1994 units surveyed survived intact while pre-1994 units suffered severe-to-catastrophic damage; assesses 60 of 67 Florida counties.
- **IBHS Charley study** (Reinhold et al.): found a "big difference" among Zone I/II/III homes, and that **80% of manufactured-home damage originated from failure of attached structures** (carports, screen rooms) that then tore into the main unit.

### D. Peer-reviewed / working-paper empirical literature
- **Simmons & Sutter (2008)**, "Manufactured home building regulations and the February 2, 2007 Florida tornadoes," *Natural Hazards* 46(3):415–425 (doi:10.1007/s11069-007-9192-4). Per the abstract: "manufactured homes built to the post-Andrew requirements were 79% less likely to be leveled than homes built prior to the HUD Code in 1976, and **68% less likely to be leveled than homes built after 1976 but before the 1994 wind load regulations**. Construction of all manufactured homes in the tornado paths to the wind load and tie-down requirements could have reduced fatalities by 70%." This is the closest thing to a clean ex post causal estimate of the 1994 standard, but it measures survival/leveling, not dollars.
- **Sutter & Simmons (2010)**, "Tornado fatalities and mobile homes in the United States," *Natural Hazards* 53:125–137 (doi:10.1007/s11069-009-9416-x) — mobile-home tornado fatality risk.
- **Broader wind-mitigation BCA literature:** Simmons & Sutter, "An economic analysis of wind resistant construction," *J. Wind Engineering & Industrial Aerodynamics* (2009); "State-by-State Analysis of Benefits to Cost from Wind-Enhanced Building Codes"; and Friedland/Rohli et al. on generalized cost-effectiveness of residential wind mitigation.
- **Flood-insurance capitalization literature** — Bin, Bishop & Kousky (2017); NFIP subsidy-capitalization studies (e.g., the nationwide repeat-sales/hedonic DiD finding average subsidy capitalization of $12,352 per property; the ZTRAX study finding a 4.2% price decline from NFIP reforms) — provides the DiD/hedonic templates the user is applying, but **none distinguishes manufactured-housing vintage pre/post-1994**.
- **Gap:** No published study monetizes NFIP flood-claim or wind-claim payout differentials across manufactured-housing vintages within the same event. The user's stated contribution appears novel.

### E. Benchmark ex ante BCRs for analogous code changes
- **NIBS/MMC "Natural Hazard Mitigation Saves" (2005)** — summarized in Rose et al., "Benefit-Cost Analysis of FEMA Hazard Mitigation Grants," *Natural Hazards Review* 8(4):97 (2007): "the overall benefit-cost ratio for FEMA mitigation grants is about 4:1, though the ratio varies from 1.5 for earthquake mitigation to 5.1 for flood mitigation"; wind grants 4.7:1. Based on ~$14B present-value benefits vs. ~$3.5B grant costs (1993–2003).
- **NIBS 2019 Report (HUD-funded)** — Table 1 and overview: "modern codes … save $11 for every $1 invested"; by peril, **the national mitigation benefit-cost ratio associated with code adoption is $6 to $1 for floods, $10 to $1 for hurricanes, and $12 to $1 for earthquakes." Building codes add "only about 1% to construction costs relative to 1990 standards.**" For **exceeding** codes: "Building along the Gulf and Atlantic coasts to comply with IBHS FORTIFIED Home requirements would cost $720 million, but save $3.8 billion per year" — a national average of **~5:1, with some state/local BCRs over 16:1**.
- **Florida Building Code (2002)** and **1994 South Florida Building Code** revisions are the standard site-built analogues; the 1992 Dade County Grand Jury drove adoption of ASCE 7-88 into the SFBC, which HUD cited as parallel justification. IBHS/FLASH studies provide component-level evidence.

### F. Industry cost estimates vs. HUD
- Historically, industry per-home estimates ran ~2× HUD's (MHI's analysis, per the Cisneros record). The same pattern persists in modern disputes: for the 2022 DOE energy rule, MHI cited ~$5,000/home (and public claims of $10,000–$15,000) versus DOE's own estimate of $627–$4,438 depending on size and climate zone. For the wind standard specifically, IBHS's Reinhold observed the Zone I-to-Zone III price gap was surprisingly small — "a couple of thousand dollars" — consistent with HUD's ~$1,500–$2,700 range and *against* the industry's doubled estimate.

## Recommendations
1. **Anchor your ex ante benchmark on the 1994 RIA:** $32.1M net annual benefit, ~1.6:1 implied BCR, per-unit cost $1,492–$2,722, and per-unit private benefit $1,516 (Zone II) / $2,022 (Zone III) at a 7% discount rate over the home's service life. State explicitly that HUD monetized property damage, FEMA relief, and VSL-based mortality, and assumed 75%/83% damage elimination.
2. **Use Simmons & Sutter (2008) as your single clean ex post physical benchmark** (68% reduction in leveling for post-1994 vs. 1976–1994 homes; 70% potential fatality reduction). Convert their damage-state reductions into implied loss reductions to compare against your insurance-claim payout differentials.
3. **Benchmark your monetized findings against NIBS BCRs** (adopting current hurricane-wind code = ~10:1; exceeding = ~5:1). If your empirical BCR lands between HUD's ~1.6:1 and NIBS's ~5–10:1, that is the expected and defensible range; a result far above 10:1 or below 1:1 should trigger a robustness re-check.
4. **Foreground the literature gap explicitly** — no prior work monetizes flood/wind claim differentials by MH vintage — to substantiate your contribution claim.
5. **Adopt the Hollar (2023) Cityscape template** for presentation: two reference homes, one-time costs vs. annual benefit streams, NPV at 3% and 7%, 30–45 year life. This aligns your paper with HUD's current methodology and makes the ex ante/ex post comparison apples-to-apples.
6. **State the thresholds that would change your conclusion:** if your DiD price effect implies capitalized benefits below the ~$1,200–$2,000/unit HUD cost, the standard fails a private cost-benefit test at the margin (though public/externality benefits could still justify it); if insurance-claim reductions exceed roughly $50–80/home/year in present value, you corroborate HUD's aggregate benefit stream.

## Caveats
- **The numeric 1994 RIA is not in the Federal Register text.** The preamble discusses costs qualitatively and states that the quantitative RIA was a separate document held for public inspection at HUD's Rules Docket Clerk office. The surviving figures come from the Eleventh Circuit opinion and HUD's 2006 re-analysis. Only the single-section Zone III pair ($2,119 production / $1,177 consumer) is directly quoted from the record; the other cells come from the 2006 restatement. The full original RIA tables (all zones, all section counts) are archived only at HUD/NARA and were not recoverable online.
- **The case is 11th Circuit (1995), not 4th Circuit** — a citation correction for the user.
- **HUD's benefit estimates are prospective model outputs**, not observed outcomes; the assumed 75%/83% damage elimination was later broadly (but not precisely) corroborated by post-storm assessments.
- **Post-storm "zero destroyed" statistics conflate the 1994 HUD construction standard with Florida's 1999 installation/tie-down standards.** Anchoring/installation, not just construction, drove much of the observed improvement (tie-down counts rose from ~10 to as many as 45). A vintage-based design should separate the federal 1994 construction standard from state installation rules.
- **NIBS BCRs are for site-built I-Code buildings and FORTIFIED retrofits, not manufactured homes specifically**, and are themselves ex ante HAZUS-based model estimates; use as order-of-magnitude benchmarks only. NIBS also cautions that its FEMA-grant BCR was later "incorrectly used to justify all types of mitigation," so match the BCR to the specific mitigation type (adopt-code vs. exceed-code vs. grant).
- **Industry vs. HUD cost estimates diverge ~2×**; report both bounds rather than adopting either uncritically.
- **Many secondary/industry web sources** (dealer blogs) repeat the "not one post-1994 home destroyed in 2004" claim; treat these as derivative of the HUD/DHSMV/Grosskopf/IBHS assessments rather than independent evidence.
